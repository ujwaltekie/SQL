Inner, Left, Right and Full Joins

Four ways to combine two tables, and exactly which rows survive each one. Every example uses one easy, visual story: students and the school clubs they've joined.

01

The four joins, at a glance

Every join here works on the same idea: try to pair a row from the left table with a row from the right table wherever the ON condition holds true. The four types only differ in what happens to a row that finds no partner. Keep this cheat sheet in mind; every section below walks through exactly one row of it, step by step.

1 of 4

Inner Join

JOIN by itself already means INNER JOIN; the word INNER is optional. It keeps only the rows where a match is found on both sides. Everything else, matched or not, is thrown away entirely.

SELECT s.name, c.name
FROM students s
JOIN clubs c
ON s.club_id = c.id;
Analogy: A raffle where only ticket numbers that were actually drawn get called out. If your ticket wasn't drawn, or a drawn number belongs to no one, neither appears on stage at all.
2 of 4

Left Join

LEFT JOIN keeps every row from the left table (the one named right after FROM), whether or not it finds a match. When there's no match, the columns coming from the right table are simply filled with NULL.

SELECT s.name, c.name
FROM students s
LEFT JOIN clubs c
ON s.club_id = c.id;
Analogy: A class attendance register. Every student's name is printed no matter what, and next to it either their club, or a blank if they haven't joined one. Nobody gets left off the register.
3 of 4

Right Join

RIGHT JOIN is the mirror image: it keeps every row from the right table, filling in NULL on the left side wherever nothing matches.

SELECT s.name, c.name
FROM students s
RIGHT JOIN clubs c
ON s.club_id = c.id;
Analogy: A list of every club a school offers, printed for the notice board, with member names filled in where they exist. A club with zero members still gets listed, just with a blank next to it.
4 of 4

Full Outer Join

FULL OUTER JOIN keeps every row from both tables. Matched rows are combined as usual; anything unmatched on either side still appears, with NULL filling whichever side is missing.

SELECT s.name, c.name
FROM students s
FULL OUTER JOIN clubs c
ON s.club_id = c.id;
Analogy: A combined guest list made by merging "people who RSVP'd" and "people who were invited", so that gatecrashers and no-shows both show up somewhere on the final sheet.
02

Joining more than two tables

Till now every join combined two tables. But real questions often need three or more. If you had to add three numbers, you'd add the first two, then add the third to that sum. Joining tables works the same way: join the first pair, then join that result with the next table.

Task: for every film, print its title, its language's name, and its original language's name. The film table links to language twice: once via language_id, once via original_language_id. So the same table is joined in twice, with two different aliases so we can tell the two lookups apart.

SELECT f.title, l1.name, l2.name
FROM film f
JOIN language l1
  ON f.language_id = l1.language_id
JOIN language l2
  ON f.original_language_id = l2.language_id;
step1 = []                                 # film joined with language (l1)
for f in film:
    for l1 in language:
        if f.language_id == l1.id:
            step1.append(f + l1)

step2 = []                                 # step1 joined with language again (l2)
for row in step1:
    for l2 in language:
        if row.original_language_id == l2.id:
            step2.append(row + l2)
Analogy: Looking up a word's meaning in a dictionary, then looking up a difficult word inside that meaning in the very same dictionary. Same dictionary, used twice, for two different purposes — hence two aliases, l1 and l2.
03

ON vs WHERE: not the same thing

Both ON and WHERE look like they "filter", so why not just write everything in WHERE?

SELECT * FROM A JOIN B ON A.id = B.id;
-- versus
SELECT * FROM A, B WHERE A.id = B.id;

The second form is secretly a CROSS JOIN: FROM A, B first builds every possible pairing of a row from A with a row from B (that's n × m rows), and only afterwards does WHERE throw away the pairs that don't match. With ON, the condition is applied while the pairing is being built, so non-matching pairs are never created at all.

⚖️ Intermediate-table size lab

A (3 rows) and B (3 rows), matching on id. Compare what each approach builds internally.

Analogy: Inviting people to a party. ON is checking each guest's invite at the door, so uninvited people never enter the venue. WHERE is letting everyone in first and only then walking around asking people to leave, using a bigger venue and more staff for no reason.
Space: ON keeps the intermediate result small; WHERE (via cross join) always builds the full n×m first.
Speed: fewer intermediate rows means less work later, so ON is generally faster.
Rule of thumb: write your join condition in ON. Reserve WHERE for filtering the joined result on things unrelated to how the tables connect (e.g. WHERE c.name = 'Music Club' after the join). Avoid a bare CROSS JOIN unless you deliberately want every pairing.
04 · Bonus

Compound Joins (a step further)

Everything above joined tables on a single equality, like s.club_id = c.id. Two more advanced ideas worth knowing, for when you're ready:

1. A join condition doesn't need to be an equality. ON can use >, BETWEEN, or any comparison.
2. ON can hold multiple ANDed conditions, exactly like WHERE. A join with more than one condition across columns is called a compound join.

Example: for every film, list other films released within 2 years of it that cost more to rent than it does.

SELECT f1.title, f2.title
FROM film f1
JOIN film f2
  ON f2.release_year BETWEEN f1.release_year - 2 AND f1.release_year + 2
  AND f2.rental_rate > f1.rental_rate;

This is still just a join: ON decides which pairs of rows get stitched together. It's simply checking a range and an inequality together, instead of a single =.

05

Quick recap

  • INNER JOIN (the default): only matched rows survive.
  • LEFT JOIN: every left-table row survives; unmatched right side becomes NULL.
  • RIGHT JOIN: every right-table row survives; unmatched left side becomes NULL.
  • FULL OUTER JOIN: every row from both sides survives, matched or not.
  • Join more than two tables by chaining ON clauses, one join at a time.
  • ON filters while the joined rows are being built (small, fast); a comma-join with WHERE builds every pairing first (a cross join, big, slow). Put join logic in ON.
  • Bonus: ON can use non-equality conditions and multiple ANDed conditions — a compound join.
